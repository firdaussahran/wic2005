How the labs work

Labs are scaffolded: you're given working code and a prepared network, and you modify it, break it on purpose, and work out what went wrong. Diagnosing a failure teaches how something works better than building it from scratch — and it saves the setup time that was never the point.

Keep a short "what we broke and why" log in your lab notes. Failure diagnosis is where the fundamentals are really learned, and it's what your lab portfolio and the alternative assessment reward.

When something won't run: copy the exact error message, not a paraphrase, and bring it to the lab session or the course forum. Week 1's Lab tab has a troubleshooting section for the most common setup problems.

Reading

Books:

  • Peterson, Cascone, O'Connor, Vachuska & Davie, Software-Defined Networks: A Systems Approach — free online at systemsapproach.org, and closest to how this course thinks.
  • Edelman, Lowe & Oswalt, Network Programmability and Automation, 2nd ed. (O'Reilly) — the practical companion.
  • Claise, Clarke & Lindblad, Network Programmability with YANGYANGA data modelling language for network configuration and state. NETCONF, RESTCONF and gNMI all carry data structured by YANG models. (Addison-Wesley) — for Weeks 8 and 9.

Papers, one for each of these weeks:

  • Feamster, Rexford & Zegura, The Road to SDNSDNSoftware-Defined Networking: taking the control plane out of individual switches and running it as software, often on a logically centralised controller. (2014) — Week 1
  • McKeown et al., OpenFlow: Enabling Innovation in Campus Networks (2008) — Week 4
  • Bosshart et al., Forwarding Metamorphosis: Fast Programmable Match-Action Processing (2013) — Week 5
  • Casado et al., Ethane: Taking Control of the Enterprise (2007) — optional, Week 4

Standards, in excerpts: RFC 6241 (NETCONFNETCONFA network management protocol, usually over SSH, that exchanges YANG-modelled data as XML and supports transactions: candidate datastore, commit and locking.), RFC 8040 (RESTCONFRESTCONFAn HTTP-based protocol that exposes YANG-modelled data as REST resources, in JSON or XML.), RFC 7950 (YANG 1.1) and RFC 8342 (NMDANMDANetwork Management Datastore Architecture (RFC 8342): separates intended configuration from running configuration and from operational state.).

Reading research papers and RFCs is a skill this course builds deliberately. You'll be told which sections to read, and they're discussed in class — you're not expected to read a standard cover to cover.

What this course doesn't cover

So that you know where to look next:

  • Vendor products as configuration courses — SD-WAN, ACI, Catalyst Center. They appear as examples of the principles, not as product training; that's for a final-year elective.
  • Software engineering in depth — testing frameworks, packaging, asynchronous programming, design patterns.
  • Cloud networking — VPCs, transit gateways, service meshes.
  • Advanced P4 — stateful data planesdata planeThe part of a network device that forwards packets, at full speed, using tables it has been given., custom externs, hardware targets.
  • Kubernetes networking — Week 6 covers the building blocks underneath it.
  • Machine learning for networks — beyond a look ahead in Week 12.
