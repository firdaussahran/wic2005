Mini-project: Operate a Network You Designed

In one sentence

In a group of 4, build a small network for a client, make every change to it go through code, and then show that it copes when something unexpected happens.

Weight15% of the course mark
Group size4 students
DueFri 1 Jan 2027 (Week 12)
Use of AILevel 3 (Open): allowed, but you must declare it — see below

The project in four steps

  1. Build a network for the client, Teras Hub, with the six parts listed below.
  2. Write down your decisions as you make them: what you chose, what else you could have chosen, and your evidence.
  3. Handle a curveball. In Week 10 you get a surprise problem. Find the cause, fix it properly, and stop it happening again.
  4. Submit your repository, a report and a recorded presentation, plus an individual reflection.

Marks go mostly to your reasoning and evidence, not to how much you build. A small network you can explain beats a big one you can't.

The client: Teras Hub

Teras Hub is a small co-working building in Cyberjaya. It rents office space, and network access, to three tenants:

  • a software start-up;
  • a university research group that uses the same IP addresses as the start-up;
  • the building's own systems (CCTV, door access, sensors), which no tenant may ever reach.

Their only network engineer is leaving. They want a network that the next person can change safely through code, and that warns them when something goes wrong. You may add tenants or details, but these three tenants, and their shared addresses, must stay.

Step 2 · Write down your decisions

Each important choice gets a short decision record (an "ADR", half a page) in docs/adr/ in your repository. Write at least six, at least one for each part C1–C6. Each record answers four questions:

  1. What did we have to decide, and why does it matter to Teras Hub?
  2. Which options did we consider? Name at least two real ones.
  3. What did we choose, and what's our evidence? A measurement, a test, a failure you caused on purpose, or a source.
  4. What does it cost? What does this choice make harder, and what would make us change it?

Write each one when you make the decision, and commit it. GitGitA version control system that records every change to a set of files as a commit, so you can see, compare and undo changes. shows when it was written.

Step 3 · Handle the curveball

In Week 10, each group privately receives a different curveball: a new request from Teras Hub, a failure, or a security incident. You can't prepare for it in advance.

What to do:

  1. Find the cause, with evidence from your running network.
  2. Compare at least two fixes, and record your choice in a decision record.
  3. Apply the fix through your pipeline. If your pipeline can't do it yet, improve the pipeline until it can.
  4. Check that it worked and that nothing else broke.
  5. Add an automatic check (a test, a validation rule or a monitor) that catches this problem next time, and show it catching the problem when you re-create it.
  6. Reflect: what was wrong in your design, and what you changed.

Not accepted as a fix. If your response stops at one of these, the curveball earns at most 1 of its 4 marks:

  • doing nothing, or saying it's out of scope;
  • switching off the part that failed;
  • a one-time change by hand, a restart or an undo, with nothing to stop it happening again;
  • a rule for people ("be careful", "don't do that") instead of a change to the system.

You may mention these quick fixes in your decision record as the option you compared against. You just can't choose one.

Step 4 · Submit (Fri 1 Jan 2027)

One member submits for the group, on Spectrum:

  1. Repository link. One documented command (or a short list of commands) rebuilds your whole network from scratch. Include your decision records, YANG model, intent files, pipeline, test results and measurements. No passwords or tokens.

  2. Report (PDF, at most 8 pages, plus appendices for raw output only, such as logs and measurements): your design and why, the C3 comparison with measurements, your security analysis, and the curveball (cause, options, fix, checks, reflection).

  3. Recorded presentation (at most 10 minutes: keep it short). Every member presents the part they owned, about 2–3 minutes each. Include one live demo: your curveball fix going through your pipeline from intent to verified, then your new check catching the problem when you re-create it. Show your screen, and show your face while you speak.

    Submit the video as a link, either:

    • Google Drive, shared so the lecturer can view it, or
    • YouTube, set to unlisted (not private).

    Open the link in a private browser window before you submit, to check it works. Don't upload the video file to Spectrum.

Each member submits individually, on Spectrum:

  1. Reflection (one page): what you owned, your best decision, one you'd change, and what you learned.
  2. Peer rating of each member's contribution, on the form provided.

Working as a group

  • Each member owns one area and presents it: C2 · C3 · C1 + C4 · C5 + C6.
  • Work in one shared repository. Commit your own work, in small steps, with clear messages. Your Git history shows who did what.
  • In your first week, agree when you'll meet and how you'll settle disagreements. Write it in the README.

Use of AI — Level 3 (Open)

You may use AI, including to help write code and text, as long as:

  • you declare it in an "AI use" section of your README: which tool, and what it did;
  • every member can explain everything you submit, including any code AI helped write;
  • your decisions are your own: AI can list options, but your choice must rest on your own evidence;
  • your results are real: every measurement, log and screenshot comes from your own network. Made-up results are misconduct.

Questions

Ask in class, or on the Spectrum forum so every group sees the answer. Send questions about your curveball to the lecturer directly.
